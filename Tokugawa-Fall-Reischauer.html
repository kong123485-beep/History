The Fall of Tokugawa Shogunate

Re-examining Reischauer's Thesis

HIST 2320 • Final PaperWord Count: 1,547
Examination Question
"Edwin O. Reischauer argues that the Tokugawa Shogunate collapsed primarily due to the external shock of Western imperialism, rather than internal decay. How valid is this thesis? Discuss with reference to the Bakumatsu period (1853-1868)."
— Source: Reischauer, E.O. Japan: The Story of a Nation (1970), Ch. 8. Cf. Jansen, Beasley, Totman.
I

Introduction — Defining the System

The question of why the Tokugawa bakufu, after 265 years of apparent stability, collapsed in less than fifteen years between Perry's arrival and the Meiji Restoration remains central to Bakumatsu historiography. Edwin O. Reischauer's classic formulation — that the Shogunate was a fundamentally sound "centralized feudalism" (Baku-han taisei) shattered by an unprecedented external shock — retains intuitive power. This essay argues, however, that Reischauer's thesis is only partially valid: it correctly identifies the timing and legitimacy crisis triggered by the West, but misdiagnoses the cause. Drawing on Conrad Totman's internal decay thesis and John Whitney Hall's conjunctural synthesis, I contend that the Bakuhan system was already a malfunctioning machine by the 1830s; Western imperialism acted as an accelerator and legitimizer of collapse, not its prime mover.

By Baku-han taisei I mean the dual polity of bakufu control over 1/4 of the land and 260 semi-autonomous han, bound by sankin-kōtai hostage residence, Confucian status hierarchy (shi-nō-kō-shō), and fiscal extraction via rice tax (nengu). Reischauer, writing in 1970 for an American audience eager to see Japan as a "modern" nation interrupted, emphasized its administrative sophistication. Yet as Totman demonstrated in The Collapse of the Tokugawa Bakufu, 1862-1868 (1980), sophistication is not solvency.

II

Why Reischauer Seems Valid: The Illusion of Stability

Reischauer's appeal rests on two observable facts. First, until 1853, the bakufu had survived every crisis — the 1651 Keian Uprising, the 1780s Tenmei famine, the 1830s Tempō crisis — without structural reform. Its bureaucracy, as Totman himself admits, was "one of the most literate and administratively competent in the early modern world." Second, the chronology appears to vindicate an external trigger: Kanagawa (1854), Ansei Treaties (1858), Sonnō jōi terror, and then, within a decade, abolition of the shogunate.

For contemporary observers like Townsend Harris, the bakufu indeed seemed a victim of unequal treaties, not its own debts. Reischauer's framework thus elegantly explains why the fall was so sudden, and why it took the form of an imperial restoration rather than a peasant revolution. If we stop in 1853, the system looks viable.

Historiographical Note

Reischauer's "modernization theory" lens — Japan as proto-modern state blocked by feudalism, then liberated by Western challenge — dominated US-Japan studies in the 1960s. It served Cold War narrative needs.

III

Why Reischauer Is Wrong: The Machinery Was Already Failing

The flaw in Reischauer is not factual but mechanical. Three interlocking failures had hollowed out Baku-han by the 1840s.

(a) Fiscal implosion. The sankin-kōtai system, requiring daimyō to spend half their revenue maintaining Edo residences and processions, created systemic debt. By 1843, the bakufu's own finances depended on forced loans (goyōkin) from Osaka merchants like Mitsui and Kōnoike; bakufu debt exceeded 70% of annual revenue. Simultaneously, han like Chōshū and Satsuma, forced to retrench, pioneered monopolies (sugar, wax) and fiscal rationalization, ironically making them richer than the center. This is not external shock; it is structural deficit.

(b) The Samurai crisis and loyalty shift. Fixed stipends in rice, paid in a monetizing economy, impoverished lower samurai (kashin, ashigaru). A Chōshū ashigaru by 1840 earned 1/3 of his 1700 purchasing power. These men became teachers, smugglers, and readers of Mitogaku and Kokugaku — schools that re-located loyalty from shogun to Emperor. The shishi activists of the 1860s were not displaced elites reacting to the West; they were products of 80 years of status-income decoupling. When Yoshida Shōin opened his Shōka Sonjuku, he taught lower samurai whose economic grievance already sought an ideological home.

(c) Class fusion and peasant upheaval. Reischauer's stable status order had dissolved. Wealthy farmers (gōnō) lent to samurai, merchants bought samurai status (gōshi), and samurai farmed. More damningly, peasant uprisings (hyakushō ikki) averaged over 20 per year 1800-1853, with more than 1,000 recorded incidents between 1800 and Perry's arrival — ten times the 18th-century rate. The bakufu's own Tempō Reforms (1841-43), an attempt to return samurai to land and suppress merchants, failed within 18 months because the market economy it sought to abolish was already the fiscal base. A system that cannot enforce its own restoration program is not sound.

IV

Why Reischauer Is Still Insightful: The West as Accelerator

To dismiss Reischauer entirely is to commit the inverse error. External pressure did not cause decay, but it determined the form and velocity of collapse in three ways.

(1) Legitimacy implosion. The Kanagawa Treaty (1854) and Ansei Treaties (1858), signed without Imperial sanction by Hotta Masayoshi and Ii Naosuke, shattered the bakufu's ideological foundation as Emperor's delegate (taikun). Emperor Kōmei's unprecedented direct intervention — his 1858 refusal to approve the treaties and his 1863 order to expel barbarians — gave Mitogaku theorists legal proof that bakufu had usurped imperial prerogative. Sonnō jōi shifted from scholarly slogan to constitutional argument. Without the treaties, shishi remain local malcontents; with them, they become loyalists.

(2) Military and financial realignment. The external crisis allowed southwestern han to re-arm with Western support: Satsuma's 1866 alliance with Chōshū was brokered by Sakamoto Ryōma using British (Glover) rifles; the bakufu, conversely, relied on French advisors (Brunet) who proved ineffective in 1868. Moreover, the treaty ports introduced a catastrophic monetary shock: Japanese gold-silver ratio was 1:5, global was 1:15. Within months, foreign merchants exported 4 million ryō of gold, causing 300% inflation in treaty ports, collapsing bakufu copper coinage, and forcing indemnities — $3 million after Shimonoseki bombardment (1864) — that bankrupted the center while enriching Satsuma-Chōshū who collected the trade taxes.

Thus the West acted as what Beasley calls "a fiscal and ideological solvent": it monetized existing contradictions.

V

Conclusion: The Malfunctioning Machine

Reischauer's machine was not struck by lightning; it was a malfunctioning machine that caught fire when exposed to a spark. The metaphor matters for historiography. Totman (1980) correctly stresses long-term internal dysfunction — fiscal crisis, samurai pauperization, and over 1,000 peasant revolts — but underplays why 1868, not 1848, became the terminal year. Jansen (Sakamoto Ryoma and the Meiji Restoration, 1961) and Beasley (The Meiji Restoration, 1972) supply the synthesis: internal decay created a pool of armed, ideologically alienated lower samurai and solvent outer han; the Western crisis gave them (a) a legitimizing cause (imperial loyalism), (b) modern arms financed by new trade, and (c) a bankrupt bakufu unable to buy them off.

Therefore Reischauer's thesis is valid in its insight — that without the West, the Tokugawa might have limped on as a decentralized fiscal regime like Qing China — but invalid in causality. The bakufu did not fall because the West arrived; it fell because when the West arrived, it had no fiscal, military, or ideological resources left to respond. The 1868 Restoration was not a revolution against the West, but a civil war within Japanese society for control over how to answer it, fought between two products of Tokugawa's own economic transformation.
